You are going to want a nicer car than you can afford. Everyone does. The
industry is built around making the expensive choice feel reasonable, and it is
very good at it.

So here is the rule, and then the reasoning, so you can decide for yourself when
it applies.

<div class="answer">
<div class="answer-label">The short answer</div>
<p>Keep the <strong>total</strong> cost of a car under <strong>35% of one year's income</strong>, and finance it for no more than <strong>four years</strong>. If you cannot do both, the car is too expensive.</p>
</div>

## Why "total cost" and not "monthly payment"

This is the part the industry depends on you getting wrong.

Nobody at a dealership will ask what you want to spend. They will ask what you
want your monthly payment to be. Those sound like the same question. They are
not, and the difference is where the money goes.

A monthly payment can be made to fit almost any budget by stretching the loan.
Seven and eight year car loans exist for exactly this reason. The payment drops,
the salesperson looks helpful, and you pay far more in the end while owing money
on a car that is falling apart.

Ask the price. Ask the interest rate. Ask the total you will have paid when the
loan ends. If someone will not answer those three questions directly, that tells
you what you need to know about the deal.

## Why four years

A car loan longer than four years is a signal, not a feature.

If you need six years to pay for something that loses value every one of those
years, you are not financing a purchase. You are renting a depreciating object at
interest, and for part of that time you will owe more than the car is worth.
That is called being underwater, and it means you cannot sell the car without
writing a check to be rid of it.

Four years is not a magic number. It is roughly the point where the loan is being
paid off faster than the car is losing value. Stay inside it and you always have
the option to sell. Options are worth more than they look.

## The real cost is depreciation, not the payment

A car is a machine that turns money into transportation, and it does this
whether you drive it or not.

New cars lose value fastest in their first years, and the steepest part of that
drop happens early. Someone else pays for that first stretch if you buy a car
that is a few years old. The car is materially the same. It has most of its life
in front of it. You simply did not pay for the privilege of being the person
whose name went on the title first.

This is the single largest lever in the whole decision. Bigger than negotiating.
Bigger than the interest rate. Buying slightly used is not a sacrifice; it is the
same product at a discount someone else already absorbed.

## What the 35% is actually protecting

The rule is not really about cars. It is about what the money would otherwise
have done.

Money spent on a car is money not invested, not saved for a house, and not
available when something goes wrong. A car that costs 60% of your income does not
just cost you the difference. It costs you every option that money represented
for as long as you are paying it off.

Nobody notices this while it is happening. That is why it needs to be a rule
rather than a judgment call, because judgment made in a showroom is not your best
judgment.

## When to bend it

Rules that never bend get discarded entirely, so here is when the number moves.

**Your income is unusually stable and you drive a great deal.** Someone who
drives 25,000 miles a year for work is buying a tool, not a toy. Reliability
becomes worth paying for, and the car earns part of its own cost.

**You are paying cash.** The four-year rule exists to limit borrowing. If there
is no loan, the constraint that matters is what else the money could have done,
which is still a real constraint but a different one.

**The cheap option is not actually cheap.** A car that needs constant repair
costs more than its price, and it costs time and reliability too. The rule sets a
ceiling, not a target. Spending less than the maximum is always allowed.

**You live somewhere a car is not optional.** Where there is no other way to get
to work, the floor on what you must spend is higher, and the rule bends to meet
it.

What does not justify bending it: the car being nicer, the payment fitting, or
the deal being good. A good deal on something you cannot afford is not a good
deal.

## The short version

Spend less than you can. Buy something a few years old. Pay it off in four years
or fewer. Then stop thinking about cars and go do something more interesting.

The point of getting this decision right is not the money. It is that a car
handled well is a decision you only have to make every eight or ten years, and a
car handled badly is a thing you think about every month for years.
